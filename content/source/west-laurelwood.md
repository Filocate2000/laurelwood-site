# West Laurelwood

## History of West Laurelwood

### 1958: The Birth of West Laurelwood

West Laurelwood emerged in 1958 as part of Tract No. 24676 during Southern California's housing boom. Home Savings & Loan Association transformed land once owned by the Fryman heirs into a mid-century suburban community. The property had been originally purchased by Chapman College in 1952 for a new campus before being sold to Home Savings & Loan.

### Late 1950s: Major Expansion

During the late 1950s, Home Savings & Loan developed the area with 290 custom homes on 5,000-square-foot lots. This development featured various mid-century architectural styles with spacious layouts, panoramic views, and cutting-edge built-in amenities. These homes attracted families seeking suburban serenity while remaining close to urban conveniences.

### 1960s-1970s: The Growth of East and West Laurelwood

As both East and West Laurelwood expanded during the 1960s and 1970s, they became highly desirable neighborhoods for families, appreciated for custom-built homes, scenic views, and proximity to nature and the city. The neighborhoods were often referred to as the "Bel Air" of the Valley.

School assignments created tension during this period. Originally, children from both East and West Laurelwood attended Carpenter Avenue Elementary School in Studio City, two miles away. By 1964, overcrowding forced East Laurelwood children to transfer to Colfax Avenue Elementary School in North Hollywood, four miles away. West Laurelwood children continued attending Carpenter with convenient bus service. Some East Laurelwood families obtained special permits to keep children at Carpenter but had to arrange their own transportation.

### 1971: The Defeat of the Laurel Canyon Freeway

In the 1970s, the proposed Laurel Canyon Freeway threatened East and West Laurelwood. This project would have cut through the Santa Monica Mountains, linking the San Fernando Valley with Los Angeles International Airport. The proposed route would have passed through key areas of both neighborhoods, altering landscapes and impacting property values.

Residents unified in opposition to the project. Councilman James Potter Jr. described it as "gerrymandering," noting the disproportionate impact on neighborhoods like West Laurelwood. Community leaders, local officials, and homeowners' associations from Studio City to the west side of Los Angeles banded together, arguing the freeway would compromise scenic hillsides, increase air pollution, and permanently alter neighborhood character.

The freeway was officially removed from the state's plans in 1971, representing a major victory that preserved the neighborhood's charm and laid-back lifestyle. This successful effort cemented West Laurelwood's reputation as a community valuing preservation and demonstrated residents' power to shape their neighborhood's future.

The proposed Laurel Canyon Freeway stretched between Slauson Avenue and Santa Monica Boulevard, with various routing options under consideration, including connections through Grey Areas, Hollywood, and Ventura Freeway junctions.

### Fryman Road Extension and the Fight for Preservation

In the early 1980s, proposals to extend Fryman Road for further residential development threatened Fryman Canyon's natural beauty. The successful opposition to the Wilacre Estates development had established precedent, and the community rallied again to safeguard Fryman Canyon.

Their efforts gained support in 1983 when the Santa Monica Mountains Conservancy purchased portions of the land to protect the canyon's ecosystem and prevent urban sprawl. A major victory was protecting the Betty B. Dearing Trail, which remains a popular hiking destination. While the Fryman Road extension was mostly halted, some portions were accepted by the City of Los Angeles for public use, allowing emergency access and utility services without compromising Laurelwood's residential character.

### 1978-1980: Preservation Efforts and Wilacre Park

In the late 1970s, a proposed 150-157 condominium development in neighboring Wilacre Estates threatened the area. Residents, collaborating with local homeowners' associations, successfully opposed the plan, leading to preservation of Wilacre Estates and Fryman Canyon as public parkland. This transformation was finalized in 1980 when Assembly Bill 990 was signed into law, protecting these areas from further development. Today, Wilacre Park provides residents access to hiking trails and scenic vistas.

## Cultural Legacy in Street Names

West Laurelwood is distinguished by street names including Spanish titles of respect such as "Doña," celebrating the region's rich cultural heritage. These names connect the neighborhood to Southern California's cultural roots, though they are not tied to historical figures. In 2001, residents successfully petitioned for the correct use of Spanish accents on street signs, further enhancing the neighborhood's unique character.

## A Glimpse into West Laurelwood's Early Development on the Big Screen

In 1964, Hollywood captured the burgeoning neighborhood in *The Disorderly Orderly*, a comedy starring Jerry Lewis. Several scenes featured West Laurelwood in its early stages, showing many unbuilt lots and homes without mature landscaping. This footage captures the neighborhood's infancy before it grew into one of Studio City's most beloved communities, providing a nostalgic look at Laurelwood's roots and preserving visual legacy of the neighborhood's early days.

## West Laurelwood Today

West Laurelwood remains one of Studio City's most sought-after neighborhoods, known for beautiful mid-century homes, peaceful streets, and strong community spirit. Many original homes have been lovingly maintained or updated with modern amenities, offering a blend of historic charm and contemporary living. Architectural diversity combined with proximity to natural beauty and urban conveniences makes West Laurelwood ideal for families and professionals.

From preserved open spaces of Fryman Canyon to scenic views of the Santa Monica Mountains, West Laurelwood offers a unique combination of tranquility and convenience. Residents continue taking pride in their community, working together to protect the neighborhood's natural environment and maintain its distinctive character.
